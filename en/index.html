Hot plasmas & fusion

Background

A plasma is a partially or fully ionised gas made of charged particles whose behaviour is collective. Often described as the fourth state of matter, it makes up most of the matter in the Sun and the stars.

Nuclear fusion seeks to reproduce the energy of the stars on Earth. A hydrogen plasma is heated to over 100 million degrees so that its nuclei fuse and release energy, with no CO₂ emissions and little long-lived waste. No material can withstand such temperatures. In a tokamak such as ITER or WEST, in Provence, the plasma is therefore held in place by powerful magnetic fields. The whole challenge is to keep it hot, stable and away from the walls for long enough.

Skills

  • Plasma physics: charged-particle dynamics, kinetic and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) models, wave propagation, instabilities and turbulence.
  • Magnetic confinement fusion: plasma equilibrium and stability, collisional and turbulent transport, heating mechanisms and confinement control.
  • Fusion technologies: how tokamaks work, heating and diagnostic systems, plasma–wall interactions and constraints on materials.

Application areas

  • Nuclear fusion: research on magnetic confinement and on improving plasma performance.
  • Fusion device engineering: design, diagnostics and optimisation of tokamak systems and components.
  • Space physics and astrophysics: study of magnetised plasmas, turbulence and magnetic reconnection phenomena.

Cold plasmas & processes

Background

Unlike the plasmas used for fusion, cold plasmas are generally out of thermal equilibrium: their electrons are very energetic, while the gas stays much cooler, sometimes close to room temperature. They can be produced at low pressure or at atmospheric pressure, notably by electrical discharges.

Their high reactivity makes it possible to etch, deposit thin films or modify the properties of a surface, often with only limited heating of the treated material. These processes are widely used in microelectronics and in industrial surface treatments.

Skills

  • Discharge physics: principles of DC, radiofrequency and microwave discharges, behaviour of plasma sheaths.
  • Experimental diagnostics: use of Langmuir probes and optical emission spectroscopy in lab sessions on different plasma sources.
  • Plasma–surface interactions: knowledge of sputtering, deposition and erosion mechanisms.
  • Processes studied in class: PECVD, magnetron sputtering and plasma etching.

Application areas

  • Microelectronics and semiconductors: etching and thin-film deposition.
  • Functional coatings: hard wear-resistant, optical or protective layers.
  • Surface treatment: nitriding, cleaning, activation before bonding or painting.
  • Health and environment: sterilisation, gas treatment.

Materials science

Background

Why does glass shatter while a metal deforms? Why do some steels resist corrosion better than others? The answer depends in particular on the composition, the internal structure of the material and its conditions of use.

Materials science seeks to understand these relationships in order to select, design or improve materials for their applications, from aircraft to food packaging. It relies on observing matter at different scales, measuring its properties (strength, hardness, wear, corrosion, heat resistance) and studying manufacturing processes. The aim is to improve the performance, reliability and lifetime of products. It is my engineering training, and the foundation of my whole career.

Skills

  • Materials science and behaviour: relationships between composition, microstructure and mechanical, thermal and chemical properties, phase transformations and degradation mechanisms.
  • Materials characterisation: analysis of microstructures, identification of phases and composition, measurement of mechanical, thermal and surface properties.
  • Processing and manufacturing: understanding of fabrication methods, heat treatments and their influence on material properties.

Application areas

  • Energy and nuclear: materials subjected to extreme conditions.
  • Transport and aerospace: alloys, composites and technical coatings.
  • Industry: materials development, process optimisation and quality control.
  • Environment and packaging: porous materials, polymers and water treatment.

Simulation & data

Background

When an experiment is too expensive, too fast, too dangerous or simply out of reach, like the core of a reactor or the space between the Sun and the Earth, it is reproduced in a computer: the physics is translated into equations, which are then solved numerically.

A simulation often produces huge amounts of results. You then need to sort them, visualise them and draw a few reliable conclusions. This double task, modelling and then analysing, is what I practised during my last two internships.

In engineering, the same approach is used to design parts: they are drawn in computer-aided design (CAD), then their strength is calculated with the finite element method before they are manufactured.

Skills

  • Numerical modelling and simulation: developing and running physical models to study complex phenomena, notably in plasmas and materials.
  • Numerical methods: solving equations, studying the stability and accuracy of calculations, simulations and parametric studies.
  • Programming and data analysis: processing, automating and visualising scientific data with Python, analysing and interpreting results.
  • Scientific computing tools: using simulation codes, Linux environments, handling large volumes of data and parallel computing.

Application areas

  • Scientific research: modelling complex phenomena, numerical simulation and exploitation of experimental results.
  • Engineering and industrial R&D: design, optimisation of products and processes, fewer experimental trials.
  • Data analysis: processing large volumes of data, automating analyses and supporting decision-making.

Research internship (associate researcher) – Laboratoire de Physique des Plasmas (LPP)

Palaiseau, France · CNRS, École polytechnique, Sorbonne Université

03/2026 – 08/2026

Subject: numerical study of forced magnetic reconnection in the solar wind.

Background

The solar wind carries plasma and magnetic fields through space. When two magnetic structures meet, their field lines can break and then reconnect in a different way, abruptly releasing energy: this is magnetic reconnection, behind solar flares and magnetic storms. I used simulation to study what happens when two bubbles of magnetised plasma are pushed against each other.

My role

  • Launch and manage simulations on a computing server with PHARE, a research code that follows ions as particles and automatically refines the mesh where the physics requires it.
  • Vary the strength of the push and the properties of the surrounding plasma to identify what controls reconnection.
  • Develop in Python the analysis pipeline for more than 300 GB of results: diagnostics, maps, animations and comparison between simulations.

Result: the push does not act directly on reconnection, but through the way the bubbles compress and form a thin current sheet. A stronger push makes reconnection about twice as fast (rate from 0.23 to 0.50).

Subject: thermodynamic calculations for modelling caesium migration in the fuel pins of sodium-cooled fast neutron reactors.

Background

In sodium-cooled fast neutron reactors, the fuel is very hot at its centre. Some elements produced by fission, such as caesium, then move along the fuel pins, which can change their behaviour. The goal was to better predict this movement.

My role

Tokamak pre-design with the D0FUS systems code

Tokamak module · IRFM × GOLEM tokamak (Prague) · February 2026 · pair project

Runaway electrons in the GOLEM tokamak

One week of remotely operated experiments on the GOLEM tokamak (Czech Technical University in Prague), the oldest tokamak still in operation in the world, and presentation of the results at IRFM.

  • Scans in gas pressure, toroidal field and chamber cleanliness.
  • Detection of hard X-ray radiation (Timepix3, scintillators) and counting of runaway electrons by Gaussian fitting in Python.
  • The toroidal field acts on the confinement of runaway electrons rather than on their generation; fewer electrons detected at higher pressure.

PPEF Master's lab session · Institut Jean Lamour (Nancy) · 2025 · pair project

Diagnostics of a radiofrequency cold plasma

Study of a 13.56 MHz capacitive discharge, of the type used in etching and deposition, by electrical measurements and optical emission spectroscopy.

  • Self-bias voltage as a function of power and pressure: ignition, hysteresis, transition from the α regime to the γ regime at higher pressure.
  • Hydrogen and nitrogen spectra: sharp rise in emission above a power threshold, bands of N2 and of the N2+ ion.
  • N2/H2 mixture: band attributed to the NH radical, a sign of reactive chemistry exploited in nitriding.

PPEF Master's lab session · Institut Jean Lamour (Nancy) · 2025 · pair project

Diagnostics of a copper electric arc

Study of a low-current arc at atmospheric pressure, initiated by separating copper contacts.

  • Electrical measurements: four phases (pre-arc, ignition by rupture of the liquid-metal bridge, steady state, extinction) and characteristic arc fluctuations.
  • Spectroscopy: emission dominated by copper (electrode erosion), self-absorption of the UV resonance lines, CuO bands at the periphery.
  • Excitation temperature of about 7,000 K using the line-ratio method, on lines chosen so as not to be self-absorbed.

PPEF Master's lab session · Institut Jean Lamour (Nancy) · 2025 · pair project

High-density helicon plasma and Langmuir probe

Langmuir probe measurement of the electron density and temperature of a low-pressure argon plasma in a helicon reactor.

  • Role of the magnetic field: confinement by the diffusion field, coupling of helicon waves by the source field, ignition thresholds and extinctions.

Experimental project · EEIGM · 2022 · team of 6

Co-rolling of dissimilar metals to lighten structures

Attempts to join Al–Cu, Ti–Cu and Ti–Al pairs by cold rolling, then brazing at 600 °C to study the interfaces.

  • Choice of pairs based on Brinell hardness tests; pre-assembly by glue, vice or Nd-YAG laser welding.
  • All the assemblies come apart in the rolling mill (30% reduction): the difference in deformation between the two metals is too large.
  • SEM analysis of the brazed joints: interdiffusion zone of about 25 µm (Ti–Al) and 80 µm (Fe–Al), with brittle intermetallics that explain the poor strength of the bonds.
